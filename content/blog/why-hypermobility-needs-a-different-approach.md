## **Understanding Hypermobility & EDS**

Hypermobility refers to joints that move beyond the typical range. In EDS and hypermobility spectrum disorders (HSD), this increased mobility comes from differences in collagen — the connective tissue that provides structure and stability.

This can lead to:

* Joint instability
* Muscle overactivity or guarding
* Chronic pain
* Frequent injuries
* Fatigue
* Proprioceptive challenges
* Digestive or autonomic symptoms
* Tension that doesn’t respond to stretching

Because the connective tissue behaves differently, the body often compensates in ways that create pain or dysfunction. Physical therapy helps address these patterns with targeted, supportive strategies.

## **1. Strengthening for Stability (The Foundation of Care)**

Strength training is the cornerstone of hypermobility management. But it must be:

* Slow
* Controlled
* Progressive
* Tailored to the individual

Key goals include:

* Improving joint stability
* Reducing reliance on passive structures
* Building endurance in stabilizing muscles
* Supporting daily movement patterns

For hypermobile patients, strength isn’t just about lifting weights — it’s about teaching the body how to hold itself safely and efficiently.

## **2. Proprioception & Neuromuscular Training**

Many people with hypermobility struggle with proprioception — the body’s ability to sense where it is in space. This can make movement feel unpredictable or unsafe.

Physical therapy uses:

* Balance training
* Closed‑chain exercises
* Controlled movement patterns
* Light resistance work/weighted movements 
* Visual and tactile cues

These strategies help the nervous system improve awareness, coordination, and confidence.

## **3. Manual Therapy (Used Thoughtfully)**

Manual therapy can be incredibly helpful — but it must be applied with intention.

For hypermobility and EDS, manual therapy may include:

* Gentle soft tissue work
* Trigger point release
* Myofascial techniques
* Nervous system calming strategies

The goal is not to “loosen” already mobile joints, but to reduce muscle guarding, improve circulation, and support more efficient movement.

## **4. Dry Needling for Pain & Muscle Guarding**

Dry needling can be a powerful tool for:

* Releasing overactive muscles
* Reducing chronic tension
* Improving neuromuscular activation
* Calming pain pathways

Many hypermobile patients experience chronic tightness that doesn’t respond to stretching. Dry needling helps reset the muscle, allowing strength and stability work to be more effective.

## **5. Movement Retraining & Functional Patterns**

Hypermobility often leads to compensatory movement patterns — the body finds “workarounds” that feel easier but aren’t efficient or supportive.

Physical therapy helps retrain:

* Gait
* Posture
* Lifting mechanics
* Core activation
* Breath coordination
* Everyday movement strategies

This creates long‑term change, not just symptom relief.

## **6. Education & Nervous System Support**

Understanding the condition is one of the most empowering parts of care.

Education may include:

* Why joints feel unstable
* Why muscles guard
* How pain works
* How to pace activity
* How to manage flare‑ups
* How to build sustainable strength

Many people with hypermobility also benefit from nervous system‑calming strategies such as breathwork, mindfulness, and gentle movement — especially when pain or fatigue are present.

## **7. Collaborative, Individualized Care**

Hypermobility and EDS are complex. No two people experience them the same way.

A comprehensive PT plan may include collaboration with:

* Rheumatology
* Pain specialists
* Pelvic floor therapists
* GI or autonomic specialists
* Mental health providers
* Yoga or movement instructors

The best outcomes come from a team approach — with physical therapy at the center.

# **The Bottom Line**

Hypermobility and EDS require thoughtful, individualized care. Physical therapy offers a comprehensive approach that supports the whole person — not just the joints.

With the right combination of strength training, neuromuscular work, manual therapy, dry needling, movement retraining, and education, people with hypermobility can feel stronger, more stable, and more confident in their bodies.

You don’t have to navigate hypermobility alone. With the right guidance, your body can feel supported, capable, and resilient.
